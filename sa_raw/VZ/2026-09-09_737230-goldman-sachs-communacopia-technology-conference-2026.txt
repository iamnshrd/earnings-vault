## Mike Ng | Analyst, Goldman Sachs
Dan, I was hoping we could just start out with a big picture strategy question. It's been just under a year since you became CEO, and you've led an incredible operational and financial turnaround of the business. Could you talk a little bit about what surprised you the most and how that's shaped how you think about the company's longer-term strategy?
## Dan Schulman | CEO, Verizon
Yeah, sure. First of all, it's great to see you, and thanks for having me, and thanks, everyone, for being here. I'd also reference the safe harbor that was up there, that I'll be at least talking about the future a little bit, and that always involves risks and uncertainties. I just wanted to point that out. It has been a year. Time flies when you're having a good time. I would say, in general, I'm pretty pleased with the progress that we've made. It's a big company. Those often take time to turn around. There were four things that I really wanted to emphasize when I came in. The first is I wanted the company to stop being overly engineering-focused and really be obsessed with our customer.
I told them I would measure our success on that on two things, was churn going down, because for the last three years, churn has been going up and I wanted to see churn come down. I also wanted to see our net adds, especially on the postpaid phone side, go up. If that happened, I figured we are starting to make progress being customer-obsessed. The second thing is I really felt like the culture at Verizon needed shaking up. We really needed to start to play to win. I am a pretty intense guy. I am very impatient. Verizon is a big company. It is bureaucratic. It is hierarchical. It is process-oriented as opposed to results-oriented. It is very risk-averse, and we were moving very slowly. I was tired of the company being more prey than predator in the market.
I basically have a mantra there that every single day matters.
We have almost 100,000 people in the company. We should be doing amazing things every single day, and that intensity, I am beginning to see that in every meeting that I go to. The third thing was I really wanted to redefine our business model. I wanted to become the most efficient telco in the world. In month two, we cut almost 13,000 people. I set what people thought were ambitious OpEx and CapEx targets. They are really not that ambitious, but $5 billion in OpEx savings, $4 billion in CapEx savings, and we will exit the year with over $5 billion in OpEx savings on a run- rate basis. I see plenty of opportunity in 2027 and 2028 to continue that. I also felt like we really needed to break through a traditional business model. The business model of most of the carriers, including Verizon, it is very subsidy-focused.
That is how you win customers, that is how you retain customers, by giving away handsets. I felt that really did not need to be the case, that we needed to redefine the rules of competition, and that we could win in the market but be very fiscally responsible. I think you are beginning to see that happen. Finally, the fourth thing is we need to have real serious returns to our investors. Now, for the past five years before I became CEO, our stock was down something like 30%-35%. We had gone from first in market cap to last in market cap. Our forward PE was one of the lowest, if not the lowest, in the industry. If you looked at our adjusted EPS free cash flow over the last five years, it compounded at - 1%.
This year we have guided now to 6%-7% for adjusted EPS, 9%-10% on free cash flow. Our revenues will go from 1.6% in Q1 to we think they will be 4% on mobile and broadband in Q4. We are likely to do three times the amount of postpaid phone net adds that we did a year ago. I would say, in general, I am pleased with the direction of travel. There is a hell of a lot more that we need to do to reach my expectations, but we're kind of on the right track right now.

I'm beginning to see churn have been going up for three years in a row, now churn is coming down year-over-year. I expect that to continue. ## Dan Schulman | CEO, Verizon
Clearly, we will be at the top- end of our guidance for that. The quarter is off to a good, strong start. Well, not really off to, it is ending on a good, strong start. ## Mike Ng | Analyst, Goldman Sachs
Great. At the beginning of the discussion, you talked about the focus on getting churn down, and 2026 has always been described as a more churn reduction-led subscriber growth year. Could you talk a little bit about what is driving the churn outlook in a little bit more detail?
## Dan Schulman | CEO, Verizon
Yeah. Well, I think churn is one of the most important metrics in our business model. I think of our business model, and I say this all the time inside the company, it's a multivariable equation. You can't be successful on one thing and think that you're doing well. It's easy to drive net adds if your cost per acquisition goes way up. Give away free phones. You can drive a lot of net adds, but that doesn't work for me. You can take churn down if you take your cost of retention way up. I want to see our churn come down and our cost of retention come down, and you're seeing that. Cost of retention's coming down double digits. Churn, I think, was down five points year-over-year in the quarter.
I expect to see our churn continue to have year-over-year improvement as we go through the rest of the year. Basically, churn for us was a lot of self-inflicted wounds. We had been raising prices for the past three years before I came in without added value. I'm not against price increases. I think price is a very important component of value, but you have to add real value if you increase prices. The first thing I told the team, and they wanted to do another price increase as soon as I came in, is I said, No, stop that. Because it's a short-term win. Your revenues go up in the short- term, and it looks like you're winning, but you're not because your churn continues to go up. It's a short-term win and a medium- to long-term disaster when you go and do that.
We stopped raising prices. Immediately, customers noticed that. We invested a lot into the end-to-end customer experience. Because customers fire companies all the time when they have a terrible experience. You may not know about it, but all of a sudden, there's a disconnect because they didn't understand the promotion, and it was too complicated. They heard one thing in a store, saw another thing on their bill, heard another thing when they called into customer service. My view on that is let's fix our end-to-end process, and then let's put out a really good value proposition in the market, track new segments, make sure it's fair and honest. Now what we're beginning to see is very sophisticated modeling going on, very sophisticated segmentation, really listening to what a customer needs, satisfying it the right way.
Hopefully you'll continue to see churn improvements and, at the same time, see our cost of retention go down.
